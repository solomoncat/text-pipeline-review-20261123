What you know about this, your full summary of the subject (checked; the Core is the lesson, the definitions, worked examples, counterexamples and deeper steps are there to take them further as they are ready; use it in your own words, never recite it, and do not announce the whole answer up front): Core: GABA, gamma-aminobutyric acid, is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; glycine has a larger inhibitory role in the spinal cord and brainstem. A presynaptic neuron releases GABA, which diffuses across the synapse and binds GABA-A receptors on another neuron. A GABA-A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel, commonly built from two alpha subunits, two beta subunits, and one gamma subunit. GABA binds at orthosteric sites, meaning the receptor’s normal agonist sites, and opens a channel permeable mainly to chloride and, to a lesser extent, bicarbonate. In many mature neurons, chloride extrusion keeps intracellular chloride low. Channel opening therefore tends to hold the membrane near the chloride equilibrium potential, often hyperpolarizing or stabilizing it and always adding a conductance that can oppose excitatory voltage changes. Benzodiazepines do not substitute for GABA, supply GABA, or simply block excitation everywhere. They bind a separate allosteric site, usually at an alpha-gamma interface, on sensitive GABA-A receptors. At ordinary therapeutic concentrations they are positive allosteric modulators: in the presence of GABA they make channel opening more frequent, increasing the inhibitory effect of the GABA that is already being released. Classical benzodiazepine sensitivity requires suitable subunits, particularly gamma 2 together with alpha 1, 2, 3, or 5; receptors containing alpha 4 or alpha 6 are generally insensitive to classical benzodiazepines. Greater inhibitory conductance reduces the probability that excitatory input will bring a neuron to threshold. Distributed across networks, this can reduce anxiety, interrupt seizure recruitment and spread, produce sedation and anterograde memory impairment, and relax muscle through actions on central neural circuits rather than directly on muscle. Repeated exposure evokes compensatory nervous-system adaptations. These can produce tolerance, meaning less effect from the same dose, and physical dependence, meaning that the adapted system functions differently without the drug. Abrupt discontinuation can therefore cause rebound anxiety, insomnia, agitation, and sometimes dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Alcohol adds central nervous-system depression, while opioids additionally suppress brainstem respiratory drive. Together with benzodiazepine-related sedation and impaired arousal, these effects can leave breathing inadequate and can be fatal.

Definitions: An agonist binds the normal receptor site and activates the receptor; a positive allosteric modulator binds a different site and increases the response to the agonist. The definition rules out describing a benzodiazepine as GABA, as a GABA-A orthosteric agonist, or as a drug that necessarily opens the channel appreciably by itself. The standard electrophysiological statement is that benzodiazepines increase the frequency of GABA-evoked GABA-A channel openings; barbiturates characteristically prolong opening duration, although both drug classes have additional actions at high concentrations. Inhibition means a reduced tendency to generate or propagate action potentials, not necessarily a more negative membrane voltage. Hyperpolarization means movement of membrane voltage further below its previous value, whereas shunting inhibition means that increased conductance makes an excitatory current produce a smaller voltage change, even when net chloride current is zero. For a single ionic pathway, current is proportional to conductance multiplied by the driving force, which is membrane voltage minus that pathway’s reversal potential; the direction follows from the sign and the charge of the ion. This model assumes a defined reversal potential and does not by itself describe bicarbonate flow, changing chloride concentrations, or network effects. Tolerance is a reduced response after repeated exposure and may differ for sedation, anticonvulsant action, and other effects. Physical dependence is an adaptation revealed by withdrawal when exposure falls. Addiction, or a substance-use disorder, instead involves impaired control and compulsive use despite harm; it is not synonymous with dependence. Respiratory depression means inadequate ventilation, often with rising carbon dioxide and falling oxygen availability, not merely feeling sleepy.

Worked examples: First, compare three conceptual experiments. With GABA alone, GABA occupies its orthosteric sites and the receptor opens. With a classical benzodiazepine alone at ordinary concentrations, little channel activity follows because there is little GABA response to potentiate. With both present, the benzodiazepine occupies its separate site and GABA evokes more frequent openings. The conclusion is increased response to endogenous inhibition, not creation of a new inhibitory transmitter. Second, calculate a chloride equilibrium potential using the Nernst approximation. At 37 degrees Celsius, for chloride it is approximately 61.5 millivolts times the base-ten logarithm of intracellular chloride concentration divided by extracellular chloride concentration. Take intracellular chloride as 10 millimolar and extracellular chloride as 110 millimolar. The ratio is 10 divided by 110, or 0.0909. Its base-ten logarithm is approximately minus 1.041. Multiplying by 61.5 millivolts gives approximately minus 64 millivolts. At a membrane voltage of minus 60 millivolts, opening chloride channels drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts. At minus 70 millivolts, it instead drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts, so there can be slight depolarization while inhibition through shunting persists. This calculation concerns chloride alone; bicarbonate makes actual GABA-A reversal potentials somewhat different. Third, use a simplified conductance-weighted voltage model. Let leak conductance be one unit with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts, and excitatory conductance be one unit with reversal potential zero millivolts. Without inhibition, the weighted voltage is minus 70 divided by two, giving minus 35 millivolts, above a threshold taken as minus 50 millivolts. Now add inhibitory conductance of three units with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts. The weighted numerator is minus 70 plus zero plus three times minus 70, which is minus 280. Total conductance is one plus one plus three, which is five. Minus 280 divided by five gives minus 56 millivolts, now below threshold. The added conductance has changed the response to the same excitation. Fourth, follow repeated use and a mixed overdose separately. During repeated use, drug-enhanced inhibition is followed by compensatory receptor and network changes; the original dose may then feel weaker, and abrupt removal can leave the adapted system excessively excitable. In an opioid combination, the opioid reduces the brainstem response to carbon dioxide, while benzodiazepine sedation can reduce wakefulness, airway protection, and the person’s ability to respond. Alcohol can add further impairment. This is not a numerical dose-addition problem, and naloxone can reverse the opioid component without reliably removing benzodiazepine or alcohol toxicity; emergency airway and ventilation support remain central.

Counterexamples: A benzodiazepine applied without GABA produces little classical potentiation, which supports rather than contradicts positive allosteric modulation; the tempting rule that every receptor ligand must activate its receptor directly is wrong. A mature neuron already at its chloride reversal potential can show no hyperpolarization when GABA-A channels open, yet excitatory voltage responses become smaller; this passes the definition of inhibition through shunting and breaks the rule that inhibition must be visible as hyperpolarization. An immature neuron with relatively high intracellular chloride can be depolarized by GABA-A activation and, in some settings, excited; this does not refute chloride-channel opening, because the direction of current depends on the chloride gradient. Glycine-mediated inhibition can reduce neuronal firing without being potentiated in the same way by a classical benzodiazepine, because glycine receptors are not GABA-A receptors; this rules out the claim that benzodiazepines strengthen every inhibitory synapse. A person prescribed a stable dose who develops withdrawal on abrupt cessation can be physically dependent without craving, escalating use, or compulsive behaviour; this separates dependence from addiction. Finally, sedation alone does not prove the same receptor action: an antihistamine can sedate without being a GABA-A positive allosteric modulator, and a sedating drug need not share benzodiazepine anticonvulsant efficacy.

Numbers: A common synaptic GABA-A receptor has five subunits, with two principal GABA-binding sites and one classical benzodiazepine-binding site, although subunit arrangements vary. Mature neuronal resting voltages are often around minus 60 to minus 70 millivolts and spike thresholds around minus 50 to minus 55 millivolts, but neither is fixed. Intracellular chloride is often roughly 5 to 15 millimolar in mature neurons, against roughly 100 to 120 millimolar outside; cell type and transport activity matter. Individual GABA-A openings last on the order of milliseconds, with no universal percentage by which a benzodiazepine increases opening frequency. Physical dependence can develop after several weeks of regular use, sometimes at prescribed doses, but there is no universal safe duration or dose.

Deeper: The conductance-based membrane model states that capacitance times the rate of voltage change equals the sum of ionic and applied currents. Each GABA-A contribution is its conductance multiplied by voltage minus the GABA-A reversal potential. Increased benzodiazepine-potentiated conductance therefore adds a voltage-dependent current and increases total membrane conductance; the latter reduces the voltage produced by a fixed excitatory current. Nernst reasoning supplies the chloride component, while bicarbonate permeability, chloride transporters such as KCC2 and NKCC1, local accumulation, and synaptic location complicate the full account. This is a case of the general principle of allostery: binding at one site changes a protein’s response at another. It is also a case of homeostatic adaptation, in which chronic alteration of signalling produces receptor uncoupling, altered subunit expression, and broader circuit compensation rather than a simple exhaustion of GABA. Subunit-selective pharmacology and mouse studies link alpha 1-containing receptors strongly to sedation and aspects of amnesia, and alpha 2 and alpha 3-containing receptors to anxiolytic and muscle-relaxant actions, but clinical effects are not cleanly one subunit per symptom. Research continues on subtype-selective modulators that might retain benefit with less sedation or dependence, and on how network adaptations maintain tolerance. Grounding notes:
Core: GABA, gamma-aminobutyric acid, is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; glycine has a larger inhibitory role in the spinal cord and brainstem. A presynaptic neuron releases GABA, which diffuses across the synapse and binds GABA-A receptors on another neuron. A GABA-A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel, commonly built from two alpha subunits, two beta subunits, and one gamma subunit. GABA binds at orthosteric sites, meaning the receptor’s normal agonist sites, and opens a channel permeable mainly to chloride and, to a lesser extent, bicarbonate. In many mature neurons, chloride extrusion keeps intracellular chloride low. Channel opening therefore tends to hold the membrane near the chloride equilibrium potential, often hyperpolarizing or stabilizing it and always adding a conductance that can oppose excitatory voltage changes. Benzodiazepines do not substitute for GABA, supply GABA, or simply block excitation everywhere. They bind a separate allosteric site, usually at an alpha-gamma interface, on sensitive GABA-A receptors. At ordinary therapeutic concentrations they are positive allosteric modulators: in the presence of GABA they make channel opening more frequent, increasing the inhibitory effect of the GABA that is already being released. Classical benzodiazepine sensitivity requires suitable subunits, particularly gamma 2 together with alpha 1, 2, 3, or 5; receptors containing alpha 4 or alpha 6 are generally insensitive to classical benzodiazepines. Distributed across networks, this can reduce anxiety, interrupt seizure recruitment and spread, produce sedation and anterograde memory impairment, and relax muscle through actions on central neural circuits rather than directly on muscle. Repeated exposure evokes compensatory nervous-system adaptations. Alcohol adds central nervous-system depression, while opioids additionally suppress brainstem respiratory drive. Together with benzodiazepine-related sedation and impaired arousal, these effects can leave breathing inadequate and can be fatal.

The definition rules out describing a benzodiazepine as GABA, as a GABA-A orthosteric agonist, or as a drug that necessarily opens the channel appreciably by itself. The standard electrophysiological statement is that benzodiazepines increase the frequency of GABA-evoked GABA-A channel openings; barbiturates characteristically prolong opening duration, although both drug classes have additional actions at high concentrations. Inhibition means a reduced tendency to generate or propagate action potentials, not necessarily a more negative membrane voltage. Hyperpolarization means movement of membrane voltage further below its previous value, whereas shunting inhibition means that increased conductance makes an excitatory current produce a smaller voltage change, even when net chloride current is zero. Counterexamples: A benzodiazepine applied without GABA produces little classical potentiation, which supports rather than contradicts positive allosteric modulation; the tempting rule that every receptor ligand must activate its receptor directly is wrong. Deeper: The conductance-based membrane model states that capacitance times the rate of voltage change equals the sum of ionic and applied currents. Each GABA-A contribution is its conductance multiplied by voltage minus the GABA-A reversal potential. Increased benzodiazepine-potentiated conductance therefore adds a voltage-dependent current and increases total membrane conductance; the latter reduces the voltage produced by a fixed excitatory current. Nernst reasoning supplies the chloride component, while bicarbonate permeability, chloride transporters such as KCC2 and NKCC1, local accumulation, and synaptic location complicate the full account. This is a case of the general principle of allostery: binding at one site changes a protein’s response at another. It is also a case of homeostatic adaptation, in which chronic alteration of signalling produces receptor uncoupling, altered subunit expression, and broader circuit compensation rather than a simple exhaustion of GABA. Subunit-selective pharmacology and mouse studies link alpha 1-containing receptors strongly to sedation and aspects of amnesia, and alpha 2 and alpha 3-containing receptors to anxiolytic and muscle-relaxant actions, but clinical effects are not cleanly one subunit per symptom. Wrong ideas: The picture of benzodiazepines as extra GABA is wrong because they bind a different site and usually require GABA to produce their characteristic potentiation. The phrase switching the brain off is wrong because effects are graded, network-dependent reductions in excitability, not universal neuronal silence. A fluent student may say more frequent opening but picture each channel as staying open longer; frequency and duration are distinct measurements. Another may say chloride enters and therefore assume that chloride always flows inward; its direction depends on membrane voltage and the chloride gradient. Topic: how benzodiazepines calm the brain
Grounding notes:
Core: GABA, gamma-aminobutyric acid, is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; glycine has a larger inhibitory role in the spinal cord and brainstem. A presynaptic neuron releases GABA, which diffuses across the synapse and binds GABA-A receptors on another neuron. A GABA-A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel, commonly built from two alpha subunits, two beta subunits, and one gamma subunit. GABA binds at orthosteric sites, meaning the receptor’s normal agonist sites, and opens a channel permeable mainly to chloride and, to a lesser extent, bicarbonate. In many mature neurons, chloride extrusion keeps intracellular chloride low. Channel opening therefore tends to hold the membrane near the chloride equilibrium potential, often hyperpolarizing or stabilizing it and always adding a conductance that can oppose excitatory voltage changes. Benzodiazepines do not substitute for GABA, supply GABA, or simply block excitation everywhere. They bind a separate allosteric site, usually at an alpha-gamma interface, on sensitive GABA-A receptors. At ordinary therapeutic concentrations they are positive allosteric modulators: in the presence of GABA they make channel opening more frequent, increasing the inhibitory effect of the GABA that is already being released. Classical benzodiazepine sensitivity requires suitable subunits, particularly gamma 2 together with alpha 1, 2, 3, or 5; receptors containing alpha 4 or alpha 6 are generally insensitive to classical benzodiazepines. Greater inhibitory conductance reduces the probability that excitatory input will bring a neuron to threshold. Distributed across networks, this can reduce anxiety, interrupt seizure recruitment and spread, produce sedation and anterograde memory impairment, and relax muscle through actions on central neural circuits rather than directly on muscle. Repeated exposure evokes compensatory nervous-system adaptations. These can produce tolerance, meaning less effect from the same dose, and physical dependence, meaning that the adapted system functions differently without the drug. Abrupt discontinuation can therefore cause rebound anxiety, insomnia, agitation, and sometimes dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Alcohol adds central nervous-system depression, while opioids additionally suppress brainstem respiratory drive. Topic: how benzodiazepines calm the brain
Grounding notes:
Core: GABA, gamma-aminobutyric acid, is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; glycine has a larger inhibitory role in the spinal cord and brainstem. A presynaptic neuron releases GABA, which diffuses across the synapse and binds GABA-A receptors on another neuron. A GABA-A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel, commonly built from two alpha subunits, two beta subunits, and one gamma subunit. GABA binds at orthosteric sites, meaning the receptor’s normal agonist sites, and opens a channel permeable mainly to chloride and, to a lesser extent, bicarbonate. In many mature neurons, chloride extrusion keeps intracellular chloride low. Channel opening therefore tends to hold the membrane near the chloride equilibrium potential, often hyperpolarizing or stabilizing it and always adding a conductance that can oppose excitatory voltage changes. Benzodiazepines do not substitute for GABA, supply GABA, or simply block excitation everywhere. They bind a separate allosteric site, usually at an alpha-gamma interface, on sensitive GABA-A receptors. At ordinary therapeutic concentrations they are positive allosteric modulators: in the presence of GABA they make channel opening more frequent, increasing the inhibitory effect of the GABA that is already being released. Classical benzodiazepine sensitivity requires suitable subunits, particularly gamma 2 together with alpha 1, 2, 3, or 5; receptors containing alpha 4 or alpha 6 are generally insensitive to classical benzodiazepines. Greater inhibitory conductance reduces the probability that excitatory input will bring a neuron to threshold. Distributed across networks, this can reduce anxiety, interrupt seizure recruitment and spread, produce sedation and anterograde memory impairment, and relax muscle through actions on central neural circuits rather than directly on muscle. Repeated exposure evokes compensatory nervous-system adaptations. These can produce tolerance, meaning less effect from the same dose, and physical dependence, meaning that the adapted system functions differently without the drug. Abrupt discontinuation can therefore cause rebound anxiety, insomnia, agitation, and sometimes dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Alcohol adds central nervous-system depression, while opioids additionally suppress brainstem respiratory drive. Together with benzodiazepine-related sedation and impaired arousal, these effects can leave breathing inadequate and can be fatal.

Definitions: An agonist binds the normal receptor site and activates the receptor; a positive allosteric modulator binds a different site and increases the response to the agonist. The definition rules out describing a benzodiazepine as GABA, as a GABA-A orthosteric agonist, or as a drug that necessarily opens the channel appreciably by itself. The standard electrophysiological statement is that benzodiazepines increase the frequency of GABA-evoked GABA-A channel openings; barbiturates characteristically prolong opening duration, although both drug classes have additional actions at high concentrations. Inhibition means a reduced tendency to generate or propagate action potentials, not necessarily a more negative membrane voltage. Hyperpolarization means movement of membrane voltage further below its previous value, whereas shunting inhibition means that increased conductance makes an excitatory current produce a smaller voltage change, even when net chloride current is zero. For a single ionic pathway, current is proportional to conductance multiplied by the driving force, which is membrane voltage minus that pathway’s reversal potential; the direction follows from the sign and the charge of the ion. This model assumes a defined reversal potential and does not by itself describe bicarbonate flow, changing chloride concentrations, or network effects. Tolerance is a reduced response after repeated exposure and may differ for sedation, anticonvulsant action, and other effects. Physical dependence is an adaptation revealed by withdrawal when exposure falls. Addiction, or a substance-use disorder, instead involves impaired control and compulsive use despite harm; it is not synonymous with dependence. Respiratory depression means inadequate ventilation, often with rising carbon dioxide and falling oxygen availability, not merely feeling sleepy.

Worked examples: First, compare three conceptual experiments. With GABA alone, GABA occupies its orthosteric sites and the receptor opens. With a classical benzodiazepine alone at ordinary concentrations, little channel activity follows because there is little GABA response to potentiate. With both present, the benzodiazepine occupies its separate site and GABA evokes more frequent openings. The conclusion is increased response to endogenous inhibition, not creation of a new inhibitory transmitter. Second, calculate a chloride equilibrium potential using the Nernst approximation. At 37 degrees Celsius, for chloride it is approximately 61.5 millivolts times the base-ten logarithm of intracellular chloride concentration divided by extracellular chloride concentration. Take intracellular chloride as 10 millimolar and extracellular chloride as 110 millimolar. The ratio is 10 divided by 110, or 0.0909. Its base-ten logarithm is approximately minus 1.041. Multiplying by 61.5 millivolts gives approximately minus 64 millivolts. At a membrane voltage of minus 60 millivolts, opening chloride channels drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts. At minus 70 millivolts, it instead drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts, so there can be slight depolarization while inhibition through shunting persists. This calculation concerns chloride alone; bicarbonate makes actual GABA-A reversal potentials somewhat different. Third, use a simplified conductance-weighted voltage model. Let leak conductance be one unit with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts, and excitatory conductance be one unit with reversal potential zero millivolts. Without inhibition, the weighted voltage is minus 70 divided by two, giving minus 35 millivolts, above a threshold taken as minus 50 millivolts. Now add inhibitory conductance of three units with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts. The weighted numerator is minus 70 plus zero plus three times minus 70, which is minus 280. Total conductance is one plus one plus three, which is five. Minus 280 divided by five gives minus 56 millivolts, now below threshold. The added conductance has changed the response to the same excitation. Fourth, follow repeated use and a mixed overdose separately. Alcohol can add further impairment. Counterexamples: A benzodiazepine applied without GABA produces little classical potentiation, which supports rather than contradicts positive allosteric modulation; the tempting rule that every receptor ligand must activate its receptor directly is wrong. A mature neuron already at its chloride reversal potential can show no hyperpolarization when GABA-A channels open, yet excitatory voltage responses become smaller; this passes the definition of inhibition through shunting and breaks the rule that inhibition must be visible as hyperpolarization. An immature neuron with relatively high intracellular chloride can be depolarized by GABA-A activation and, in some settings, excited; this does not refute chloride-channel opening, because the direction of current depends on the chloride gradient. Glycine-mediated inhibition can reduce neuronal firing without being potentiated in the same way by a classical benzodiazepine, because glycine receptors are not GABA-A receptors; this rules out the claim that benzodiazepines strengthen every inhibitory synapse. A person prescribed a stable dose who develops withdrawal on abrupt cessation can be physically dependent without craving, escalating use, or compulsive behaviour; this separates dependence from addiction. Finally, sedation alone does not prove the same receptor action: an antihistamine can sedate without being a GABA-A positive allosteric modulator, and a sedating drug need not share benzodiazepine anticonvulsant efficacy.

Topic: how benzodiazepines calm the brain
Grounding notes:
Core: GABA, gamma-aminobutyric acid, is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; glycine has a larger inhibitory role in the spinal cord and brainstem. A presynaptic neuron releases GABA, which diffuses across the synapse and binds GABA-A receptors on another neuron. A GABA-A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel, commonly built from two alpha subunits, two beta subunits, and one gamma subunit. GABA binds at orthosteric sites, meaning the receptor’s normal agonist sites, and opens a channel permeable mainly to chloride and, to a lesser extent, bicarbonate. In many mature neurons, chloride extrusion keeps intracellular chloride low. Channel opening therefore tends to hold the membrane near the chloride equilibrium potential, often hyperpolarizing or stabilizing it and always adding a conductance that can oppose excitatory voltage changes. Benzodiazepines do not substitute for GABA, supply GABA, or simply block excitation everywhere. They bind a separate allosteric site, usually at an alpha-gamma interface, on sensitive GABA-A receptors. At ordinary therapeutic concentrations they are positive allosteric modulators: in the presence of GABA they make channel opening more frequent, increasing the inhibitory effect of the GABA that is already being released. Classical benzodiazepine sensitivity requires suitable subunits, particularly gamma 2 together with alpha 1, 2, 3, or 5; receptors containing alpha 4 or alpha 6 are generally insensitive to classical benzodiazepines. Greater inhibitory conductance reduces the probability that excitatory input will bring a neuron to threshold. Distributed across networks, this can reduce anxiety, interrupt seizure recruitment and spread, produce sedation and anterograde memory impairment, and relax muscle through actions on central neural circuits rather than directly on muscle. Repeated exposure evokes compensatory nervous-system adaptations. These can produce tolerance, meaning less effect from the same dose, and physical dependence, meaning that the adapted system functions differently without the drug. Abrupt discontinuation can therefore cause rebound anxiety, insomnia, agitation, and sometimes dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Alcohol adds central nervous-system depression, while opioids additionally suppress brainstem respiratory drive. Together with benzodiazepine-related sedation and impaired arousal, these effects can leave breathing inadequate and can be fatal.

Definitions: An agonist binds the normal receptor site and activates the receptor; a positive allosteric modulator binds a different site and increases the response to the agonist. The definition rules out describing a benzodiazepine as GABA, as a GABA-A orthosteric agonist, or as a drug that necessarily opens the channel appreciably by itself. The standard electrophysiological statement is that benzodiazepines increase the frequency of GABA-evoked GABA-A channel openings; barbiturates characteristically prolong opening duration, although both drug classes have additional actions at high concentrations. Inhibition means a reduced tendency to generate or propagate action potentials, not necessarily a more negative membrane voltage. Hyperpolarization means movement of membrane voltage further below its previous value, whereas shunting inhibition means that increased conductance makes an excitatory current produce a smaller voltage change, even when net chloride current is zero. For a single ionic pathway, current is proportional to conductance multiplied by the driving force, which is membrane voltage minus that pathway’s reversal potential; the direction follows from the sign and the charge of the ion. This model assumes a defined reversal potential and does not by itself describe bicarbonate flow, changing chloride concentrations, or network effects. Tolerance is a reduced response after repeated exposure and may differ for sedation, anticonvulsant action, and other effects. Physical dependence is an adaptation revealed by withdrawal when exposure falls. Addiction, or a substance-use disorder, instead involves impaired control and compulsive use despite harm; it is not synonymous with dependence. Respiratory depression means inadequate ventilation, often with rising carbon dioxide and falling oxygen availability, not merely feeling sleepy.

Worked examples: First, compare three conceptual experiments. With GABA alone, GABA occupies its orthosteric sites and the receptor opens. With a classical benzodiazepine alone at ordinary concentrations, little channel activity follows because there is little GABA response to potentiate. With both present, the benzodiazepine occupies its separate site and GABA evokes more frequent openings. The conclusion is increased response to endogenous inhibition, not creation of a new inhibitory transmitter. Second, calculate a chloride equilibrium potential using the Nernst approximation. At 37 degrees Celsius, for chloride it is approximately 61.5 millivolts times the base-ten logarithm of intracellular chloride concentration divided by extracellular chloride concentration. Take intracellular chloride as 10 millimolar and extracellular chloride as 110 millimolar. The ratio is 10 divided by 110, or 0.0909. Its base-ten logarithm is approximately minus 1.041. Multiplying by 61.5 millivolts gives approximately minus 64 millivolts. At a membrane voltage of minus 60 millivolts, opening chloride channels drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts. At minus 70 millivolts, it instead drives voltage toward minus 64 millivolts, so there can be slight depolarization while inhibition through shunting persists. This calculation concerns chloride alone; bicarbonate makes actual GABA-A reversal potentials somewhat different. Third, use a simplified conductance-weighted voltage model. Let leak conductance be one unit with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts, and excitatory conductance be one unit with reversal potential zero millivolts. Without inhibition, the weighted voltage is minus 70 divided by two, giving minus 35 millivolts, above a threshold taken as minus 50 millivolts. Now add inhibitory conductance of three units with reversal potential minus 70 millivolts. The weighted numerator is minus 70 plus zero plus three times minus 70, which is minus 280. Total conductance is one plus one plus three, which is five. Minus 280 divided by five gives minus 56 millivolts, now below threshold. The added conductance has changed the response to the same excitation. Fourth, follow repeated use and a mixed overdose separately. During repeated use, drug-enhanced inhibition is followed by compensatory receptor and network changes; the original dose may then feel weaker, and abrupt removal can leave the adapted system excessively excitable. In an opioid combination, the opioid reduces the brainstem response to carbon dioxide, while benzodiazepine sedation can reduce wakefulness, airway protection, and the person’s ability to respond. Alcohol can add further impairment. This is not a numerical dose-addition problem, and naloxone can reverse the opioid component without reliably removing benzodiazepine or alcohol toxicity; emergency airway and ventilation support remain central.

Counterexamples: A benzodiazepine applied without GABA produces little classical potentiation, which supports rather than contradicts positive allosteric modulation; the tempting rule that every receptor ligand must activate its receptor directly is wrong. A mature neuron already at its chloride reversal potential can show no hyperpolarization when GABA-A channels open, yet excitatory voltage responses become smaller; this passes the definition of inhibition through shunting and breaks the rule that inhibition must be visible as hyperpolarization. An immature neuron with relatively high intracellular chloride can be depolarized by GABA-A activation and, in some settings, excited; this does not refute chloride-channel opening, because the direction of current depends on the chloride gradient. Glycine-mediated inhibition can reduce neuronal firing without being potentiated in the same way by a classical benzodiazepine, because glycine receptors are not GABA-A receptors; this rules out the claim that benzodiazepines strengthen every inhibitory synapse. A person prescribed a stable dose who develops withdrawal on abrupt cessation can be physically dependent without craving, escalating use, or compulsive behaviour; this separates dependence from addiction. Finally, sedation alone does not prove the same receptor action: an antihistamine can sedate without being a GABA-A positive allosteric modulator, and a sedating drug need not share benzodiazepine anticonvulsant efficacy.

Numbers: A common synaptic GABA-A receptor has five subunits, with two principal GABA-binding sites and one classical benzodiazepine-binding site, although subunit arrangements vary. Mature neuronal resting voltages are often around minus 60 to minus 70 millivolts and spike thresholds around minus 50 to minus 55 millivolts, but neither is fixed. Intracellular chloride is often roughly 5 to 15 millimolar in mature neurons, against roughly 100 to 120 millimolar outside; cell type and transport activity matter. Individual GABA-A openings last on the order of milliseconds, with no universal percentage by which a benzodiazepine increases opening frequency. Plasma half-life is not duration of receptor action: alprazolam is often around 11 hours in healthy adults, lorazepam roughly 10 to 20 hours, and diazepam commonly one to two days, with an active metabolite persisting for several days. Age, liver disease, interactions, and formulation widen these ranges. Physical dependence can develop after several weeks of regular use, sometimes at prescribed doses, but there is no universal safe duration or dose.

Deeper: The conductance-based membrane model states that capacitance times the rate of voltage change equals the sum of ionic and applied currents. Each GABA-A contribution is its conductance multiplied by voltage minus the GABA-A reversal potential. Increased benzodiazepine-potentiated conductance therefore adds a voltage-dependent current and increases total membrane conductance; the latter reduces the voltage produced by a fixed excitatory current. Nernst reasoning supplies the chloride component, while bicarbonate permeability, chloride transporters such as KCC2 and NKCC1, local accumulation, and synaptic location complicate the full account. This is a case of the general principle of allostery: binding at one site changes a protein’s response at another. It is also a case of homeostatic adaptation, in which chronic alteration of signalling produces receptor uncoupling, altered subunit expression, and broader circuit compensation rather than a simple exhaustion of GABA. Subunit-selective pharmacology and mouse studies link alpha 1-containing receptors strongly to sedation and aspects of amnesia, and alpha 2 and alpha 3-containing receptors to anxiolytic and muscle-relaxant actions, but clinical effects are not cleanly one subunit per symptom. Research continues on subtype-selective modulators that might retain benefit with less sedation or dependence, and on how network adaptations maintain tolerance. The exact physiological interaction in mixed overdoses varies with drugs, dose, tolerance, airway state, and underlying illness; it is not one universally quantifiable synergy.

Further: Are benzodiazepines the same as barbiturates? No; both can potentiate GABA-A signalling, but benzodiazepines characteristically increase opening frequency, whereas barbiturates prolong openings and can directly gate receptors at higher concentrations, contributing to their narrower safety margin. Are Z-drugs such as zolpidem unrelated? No; they act at the benzodiazepine site, with differing subunit preferences, and can still cause amnesia, complex sleep behaviour, dependence, and dangerous combinations. Why do drugs with similar receptor actions last differently? Absorption, distribution, lipid solubility, metabolism, active metabolites, and elimination half-life differ, so half-life is not simply potency. Why can someone become agitated or disinhibited? Network-level disinhibition and impaired control can produce paradoxical behaviour without GABA-A receptors becoming excitatory benzodiazepine targets. Why is memory affected? Increased inhibition in memory-encoding networks can impair formation of new memories, especially anterograde memory, rather than erase an established past. Does sedation mean restorative natural sleep? No; drug-induced sedation changes sleep architecture and can coexist with poor daytime functioning. Should someone stop abruptly once dependence is suspected? No; after sustained use, an individualized, medically supervised gradual reduction is generally used because abrupt withdrawal can be dangerous. What happens in a suspected mixed overdose? Emergency help, airway support, ventilation, and monitoring are essential; naloxone is important when opioids are involved, while flumazenil is not a routine household antidote because it can precipitate seizures and other complications. Do oral GABA supplements reproduce benzodiazepines? No; GABA crosses the blood-brain barrier poorly and does not act as a selective positive allosteric modulator. Are all benzodiazepines interchangeable? No; their pharmacokinetics, approved uses, formulations, and clinical risks differ even though the core receptor mechanism is shared.

Wrong ideas: The picture of benzodiazepines as extra GABA is wrong because they bind a different site and usually require GABA to produce their characteristic potentiation. The phrase switching the brain off is wrong because effects are graded, network-dependent reductions in excitability, not universal neuronal silence. A fluent student may say more frequent opening but picture each channel as staying open longer; frequency and duration are distinct measurements. Another may say chloride enters and therefore assume that chloride always flows inward; its direction depends on membrane voltage and the chloride gradient. It is also wrong to equate depolarization with excitation in every case, because depolarizing GABA can still inhibit through shunting, although sufficiently strong depolarization can excite.
